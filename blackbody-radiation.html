Historical Background

In the late 19th century, physicists grappled with modeling thermal radiation from idealized "blackbodies", objects that absorb all incident light and, in thermal equilibrium, emit a characteristic spectrum. Although experiments around 1860 yielded the first quantitative measurements of blackbody spectra[1], a theoretical description did not emerge until Lord Rayleigh and James Jeans derived the Rayleigh–Jeans law at the start of the 20th century. While this law matched observations at long wavelengths, it predicted an unphysical divergence in the ultraviolet, and this divergence was later dubbed the "ultraviolet catastrophe." In 1900, Max Planck resolved this discrepancy by postulating that energy is emitted in discrete quanta proportional to frequency, yielding Planck’s law[2], which reproduced the Rayleigh–Jeans behavior at long wavelengths while also resolving the ultraviolet catastrophe.

In this lab, you will fit Planck's Law to the solar spectrum to calculate the surface temperature of the sun.

Theory

An ideal blackbody is an object that absorbs all incident electromagnetic radiation, regardless of frequency or angle of incidence, and re-emits energy in a characteristic spectrum that depends only on its temperature. In thermal equilibrium, the power per unit area per unit wavelength, often called the spectral radiance $B_{\lambda}(T)$, was derived from classical arguments to be

$$\begin{equation} B_{\lambda}(T) = \frac{2ck_BT}{\lambda^4} \,.\label{eq:rayleigh-jeans} \end{equation}$$

This is the Rayleigh-Jeans Law discussed in the historical background above. Note, however, that this radiated power diverges in the limit $\lambda \rightarrow 0$—the aforementioned ultraviolet catastrophe. This was in complete contradiction with experimental measurements, which showed that emission actually falls off at short wavelengths.

Planck resolved this discrepancy by introducing the hypothesis that each electromagnetic mode of frequency $\nu$ can only exchange energy in discrete quanta of magnitude $h\nu$, where $h$ is now known as Planck's constant. This approach led to what we now call Planck's Law:

$$\begin{equation} B_{\lambda}(T) = \frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\left[\exp{\left( \frac{hc}{\lambda k_B T} \right)} - 1\right]^{-1} \,.\label{eq:planck-law} \end{equation}$$

Here, $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant. At long wavelengths, this equation reduces to the Rayleigh-Jeans law, while at short wavelengths the exponential in the denominator enforces a rapid decay of emitted intensity.

Derivation 1

An ideal blackbody is a body that absorbs all incident radiation and emits a characteristic spectrum that depends only on its temperature at thermal equilibrium. A convenient way to realize this is via cavity radiation: imagine a perfectly reflecting box of volume $V$ maintained at temperature $T$ with only a tiny hole in one wall. Any light entering that hole undergoes so many internal reflections that it is effectively trapped and fully absorbed, making the interior radiation come into equilibrium with the walls. To derive the Planck function, we consider the electromagnetic field inside that cavity. The boundary conditions enforce standing-wave patterns, which can be viewed as a large collection of independent harmonic oscillators (one for each allowed wavelength $\lambda$). Planck's novel contribution here was to postulate that each oscillator can exchange energy only in discrete 'quanta' of size $h\nu=hc/\lambda$.

Consider such a box (cavity) of side length $L$. In this box, the standing-wave solutions have wave vectors

$$\vec{k} = (k_x, k_y, k_z)\,, \tag{D1.1}$$

with each component an integer multiple of $\pi/L$. In $k$-space, these allowed points form a cubic lattice of spacing $\pi/L$, so the total number of modes with magnitude between $k$ and $k + dk$ is

$$dN_k = \frac{2}{8}\times \frac{(\textrm{volume of shell of radius }k)}{(\textrm{volume per lattice point})} = \frac{2}{8}\times\frac{4\pi k^2\,dk}{(\pi/L)^3} = \frac{\pi V}{\pi^3}k^2\,dk = \frac{V}{\pi^2}k^2\,dk \,. \tag{D1.2}$$

Here, the factor of $2$ comes from accounting for both polarizations of light, and the factor of $1/8$ comes from only taking the octant where all wave numbers $k$ are positive. Since $k=2\pi/\lambda$, we can change variables using $dk = -2\pi/\lambda^2\,d\lambda$, so that in magnitude:

$$ \frac{dN_{\lambda}}{V} = \frac{8\pi}{\lambda^4}\,d\lambda \,. \tag{D1.3}$$

Next, we take each mode of wavelength $\lambda$ to be a quantum harmonic oscillator with energy levels

$$ E_n = n\frac{hc}{\lambda}\,, \hspace{0.5cm} n=0,1,2,... \,, \tag{D1.4}$$

and the probability of level $n$ is proportional to $\exp{\left[ -E_n / (k_BT) \right]}$ (this is something you'll learn in statistical mechanics). We can then take effectively a weighted or ensemble average to find the mean energy of such an oscillator to be (using properties of geometric series)

$$ \langle E \rangle = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\frac{hc}{\lambda}e^{-nhc/(\lambda k_BT)}}{\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-nhc/(\lambda k_BT)}} = \frac{hc/\lambda}{e^{hc/(\lambda k_BT)}-1} \,. \tag{D1.5}$$

Multiplying this average energy per mode by the modes per volume per $d\lambda$ (e.g. $dN_{\lambda}/V$) gives the energy per volume for wavelengths between $\lambda$ and $\lambda + d\lambda$

$$ u(\lambda,T)\,d\lambda = \frac{dN_{\lambda}}{V}\times \langle E \rangle = \frac{8\pi h c}{\lambda^5} \frac{1}{e^{hc/(\lambda k_BT)}-1}\,d\lambda \,. \tag{D1.6}$$

Finally, the spectral radiance $B_{\lambda}(T)$ (power per unit area per unit solid angle per unit wavelength) follows by noting that an isotropic energy density $u$ streams out at speed $c$ over solid angle $4\pi$ steradians, so

$$\boxed{B_{\lambda}(T) = \frac{c}{4\pi}u(\lambda,T) = \frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\frac{1}{e^{hc/(\lambda k_BT)}-1}} \,. \tag{D1.7}$$

Planck's law perfectly captures the spectrum of blackbody radiation, which is produced by a variety of different objects to varying degrees. Examples of near-perfect blackbodies include stars, tungsten filaments in incandescent light bulbs, and electric stove burners. Note that blackbody radiation usually arises in combination with other processes that further modify the observed spectrum; however, in this lab, we will only be working with Planck's Law.

Pre-Lab Questions

Submit your responses to the following questions by the start of the relevant lab meeting.

  1. The solar spectrum looks very close to that of a perfect blackbody but with some modifications, the most pronounced of which are sharp lines that dip below the blackbody curve. What is the source of these dips?

  2. Just by visual inspection of the solar spectrum, around what wavelength does the sun's emission peak? In what region of the EM spectrum does this wavelength sit (e.g. ultraviolet, visible, infrared, radio, etc.)? If it is visible, what color does this correspond to?

  3. Show that for large wavelengths, Planck's Law approximates the Rayleigh-Jeans Law. Note that, for small $x$, $e^x \sim (1+x)$.

  4. The wavelength at which the blackbody spectrum peaks is given by Wien's Displacement Law, $\lambda_{\mathrm{peak}} = b/T$, where $b$ is a constant, and $T$ is the temperature in $K$. Derive this law by finding the wavelength at which the spectrum is maximized, and find the value of the constant $b$. This can be done by differentiating Planck's Law and equating it to zero, although you will ultimately need to numerically solve the resultant equation.

  5. The Stefan-Boltzmann Law $P=\sigma A T^4$ describes the total power output from an ideal blackbody of surface area $A$ at temperature $T$ (here, $\sigma$ is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant). Derive this law by integrating $\pi$ times the Planck function over all wavelengths ($0$ to $\infty$). Note that this factor of $\pi$ arises for geometric reasons outside the scope of this problem. You may find the following integral useful: $\int_0^{\infty} x^3/(e^x-1)\,dx=\pi^4/15$.

Procedure

Fit the Planck function to this solar spectrum data to determine the surface temperature of the sun.
